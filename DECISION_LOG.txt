ENTRY 001  |  2026-08-10  |  Why this paper exists, and why it replaces an earlier one
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
An earlier version of this research had drifted into a data-quality paper about
broken public datasets. That work was rigorous and has been preserved separately,
but it answered a question nobody in this project had asked. The actual question,
the one that motivated the collaboration, is a product question with a research
core: Dr. Anderson has written a book on fitness, health and habit formation, and
a companion web application exists that logs a user's profile, goals and daily
behaviour. The feature he wants is recommendation. Given what the application
knows about a person, what should it suggest they do next?

DECISION.
Restart the paper around that question. Keep three assets from the earlier work
because they are directly load-bearing here, and discard the rest of the framing.
  1. The NHANES analysis. It established, on 5,929 adults, that BMI category
     explains about 64 percent of the variance in waist-to-height ratio but under
     3 percent of the variance in weekly physical activity, and that waist-to-
     height ratio alone predicts guideline adherence better (AUC 0.633) than BMI
     in either categorical (0.588) or continuous (0.598) form. That is the
     empirical case for why a recommender must not key on BMI, and it is now
     foundational rather than a closing remark.
  2. The dataset screen. A harness that detects when a modelling target is an
     exact function of a single other column. It is now a tool for vetting
     training data rather than the subject of the paper. Every dataset admitted
     to this study must pass it.
  3. The finding that motivated the screen in the first place: a widely
     downloaded Kaggle "fitness recommendation" dataset with 80,000 rows turned
     out to contain 16 distinct records, with every exercise and meal
     recommendation an exact function of BMI category. This is the strawman the
     new paper argues against, and we now have proof it exists in the wild.

RATIONALE.
The intellectual honesty of the earlier work is the reason it is worth reusing.
We are not asserting that BMI-keyed recommendation is inadequate; we measured it.

NEXT.
Acquire real data. Two parallel searches: per-person longitudinal behavioural
logs, and cross-sectional profile-plus-outcome datasets. Screen everything for
fabrication before admitting it.

ENTRY 002  |  2026-08-10  |  The central methodological obstacle, stated up front
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
There is no public dataset that records "person P should have been told to do X."
That label does not exist. This is not an oversight in the data landscape; it is
a structural fact. The correct recommendation for an individual is a
counterfactual, and counterfactuals are not logged.

This is precisely why the fabricated dataset described in ENTRY 001 exists:
somebody needed recommendation labels in order to train a classifier, could not
find any, and generated them from BMI. The resulting file trains a model that
reports 89 percent accuracy and has learned a four-line lookup table.

DECISION.
Do not attempt to predict "the correct recommendation" from data. Instead build
the study around quantities that ARE observable in real logs:
  - whether a person actually performed an activity (adherence),
  - what their behaviour was on the following day or week (proximal outcome),
  - and how those relate to their profile and history.
A recommendation is then chosen as the permissible action with the highest
expected adherence-weighted benefit, rather than read off a label.

RATIONALE.
Every claim in the paper must be checkable against something that was really
measured. Predicting an invented label is the failure mode we already documented
in the previous paper, and repeating it here would be indefensible.

NEXT.
Confirm, from the acquired data, that the observable quantities above are in fact
present and modellable. If per-person longitudinal data with adherence signals
does not exist publicly at usable scale, the design must change, and that
constraint will be reported rather than hidden.

ENTRY 003  |  2026-08-10  |  Reading the book, and finding the paper inside it
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
Read Dr. Anderson's book source (WELLNESSHUB4/Book.md, 1,942 lines) directly.
It is not a narrative book so much as a structured specification: five wellness
domains, a prevention index that scores fourteen health behaviours by importance,
five ten-item wellness questionnaires with a fixed scoring rubric, food group
targets, serving-size definitions, activity guidance, heart-rate zones, an RPE
scale, sleep and fluid targets, caloric needs, a four-step goal-setting
framework, strength-training progression, and contraindications.

The decisive find is section 56, titled "HARDCODED RECOMMENDATION LOGIC"
(Book.md lines 1626 to 1731). The book already contains a recommendation engine,
written as pseudocode. Its structure is:

    food servings      = f(age band, sex)
    daily calories     = f(age band, sex, activity level)
    activity minutes   = a fixed constant for everyone
    heart-rate zones   = f(age)
    sleep and water    = fixed constants for everyone

In other words, the recommendation is an exact function of three demographic
variables, and for several outputs it is a constant. Nothing a user logs after
onboarding changes what they are told.

DECISION.
This is the research question, and it is much better than the one I was going to
invent. The paper should ask: how much personalization does a health behaviour
recommendation actually need, and where do the returns to personalization
appear? Dr. Anderson's engine is not a strawman we made up. It is a real,
guideline-faithful, deployed-intent system written by a domain expert, and it is
the honest baseline. The fabricated Kaggle dataset from the earlier work is the
same structural pattern taken to its pathological extreme: recommendations as an
exact function of BMI category alone, with sixteen distinct outputs for eighty
thousand people.

We therefore define a PERSONALIZATION LADDER and measure each rung:

    L0  Population constant        one recommendation for everyone
                                   (the book's activity, sleep, water targets)
    L1  Demographic lookup         f(age, sex, activity level)
                                   (the book's food and calorie engine)
    L2  Anthropometric and risk    add waist, waist-to-height, conditions,
                                   readiness screening
    L3  Behaviour-adaptive         add the person's own logged history:
                                   their baseline, trend, variability, adherence
    L4  Context-adaptive           add within-day and within-week context,
                                   learned online

RATIONALE.
Every rung is implementable, every rung is a real design choice somebody has to
make when building this app, and the difference between rungs is measurable
against observed behaviour. This turns a vague question ("how should we
recommend?") into a quantitative one ("how much of what we need to know is
captured at each level?"). It also means the paper's conclusion is directly
actionable for the product: it tells Dr. Anderson which rung to build.

The earlier NHANES result is now the first evidence on the ladder. BMI category,
an L1-style variable, explains under 3 percent of the variance in how much
activity a person actually does. If that pattern holds in the new data, the
conclusion is that L1 cannot personalize behaviour, only body-size-linked
quantities, and that the useful signal starts at L3.

NEXT.
Confirm the ladder is measurable in the acquired data. Specifically we need,
at minimum, a dataset with per-person repeated observations so that L3 can be
constructed at all, and a cross-sectional dataset with both demographics and
behaviour so that L1 and L2 can be compared. Two acquisition agents are running.

ENTRY 004  |  2026-08-10  |  Additional structure available from the book
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
Three further parts of the book are directly usable as research material rather
than background.

1. The prevention index (Book.md lines 64 to 87) assigns each of fourteen health
   behaviours a numeric importance score from 6.7 to 9.8, with not smoking at
   9.8 and sleep at 6.7. This is an explicit, expert-authored utility function
   over behaviours. Recommendation requires exactly such a utility in order to
   rank competing suggestions, and most systems have to invent one. We have one,
   authored by the domain expert who is a co-author.

2. The wellness questionnaires (lines 87 to 167) define five ten-item scales
   with a fixed scoring rubric and five interpretation bands. This is a
   ready-made assessment instrument that maps a person onto an interpretable
   state vector.

3. The adherence factors (lines 787 to 815) name predisposing, enabling and
   reinforcing determinants of whether somebody sticks with activity. This is
   the classic PRECEDE framework, and it tells us which features the adherence
   model should contain.

4. The contraindications (lines 1813 to 1834) give the refusal conditions: chest
   pain, persistent high resting heart rate, unexplained weight change, chronic
   insomnia, suspected depression. A recommender must be able to decline and
   defer to a clinician, and these are the triggers.

DECISION.
Encode all four as machine-readable resources in the repository: a prevention
index table, the questionnaire scoring, the adherence feature list, and a
refusal rule set. These become the knowledge layer of the system and are
citable to the book rather than invented by us.

RATIONALE.
It keeps the boundary honest between what is asserted by domain expertise and
what is learned from data. Anything in the knowledge layer traces to the book or
to a published guideline. Anything in the learned layer traces to a measured
result. The paper can then be explicit about which is which, which is precisely
the criticism the previous paper attracted and survived.

NEXT.
Wait for dataset acquisition, then match the ladder rungs to the columns that
actually exist.

ENTRY 005  |  2026-08-10  |  First measured result, and it is a surprising one
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
Implemented the book's engine exactly as written (src/01_knowledge_base.py) and
enumerated its entire output space over a realistic adult input grid: ages 18 to
80, two sexes, three activity levels, so 378 distinct user types.

    distinct diet prescriptions                    6
    distinct activity / sleep / water prescriptions 1
    distinct full recommendations                  14
    compression (outputs / inputs)                 0.037
    share of the input grid with a defined
      calorie target                               19.0%

Two things stand out. First, the activity, sleep and water targets are a single
constant across all 378 user types: every adult is told 30 minutes on 5 days,
7 to 8 hours of sleep, 6 to 8 cups of water. Second, the calorie rule is defined
for only 19 percent of the grid, because the book gives the 18-to-35 band and
then writes "[Continue pattern for other age ranges...]". The deployed engine is
literally undefined for four fifths of its own input space.

THE COMPARISON THAT MATTERS.
The fabricated Kaggle dataset from the previous paper contained 16 distinct
recommendations for 80,000 people. Dr. Anderson's expert-authored engine contains
14 distinct recommendations for the entire adult population.

Structurally these are the same object: a small lookup table keyed on a few
demographic variables. The difference is provenance, not architecture. The book's
14 are traceable to Canadian and WHO guidelines and are safe. The dataset's 16
were invented. But neither adapts to anything a person does.

DECISION.
Open the paper with this. It is a far stronger motivation than criticizing an
anonymous Kaggle upload, because it shows that the lookup-table pattern is not a
symptom of bad data hygiene. It is what careful, well-intentioned, guideline-
faithful design produces by default, because population guidelines are written
for populations. The gap between a guideline and a recommendation for one person
is the research problem.

I want to be careful and fair here, and the paper must be too. This is not a
criticism of the book. The book is doing exactly what a book can do. The point is
that an application has information a book never had: what this specific person
actually did yesterday, last week, and last month. The question is whether that
information is worth anything, and that is an empirical question we can answer.

RATIONALE.
It also protects the paper from an obvious referee objection. If we only compared
against a strawman we would be accused of knocking down a dataset nobody defends.
Comparing against a real expert system authored by the second author is a much
harder test and a more honest one.

NEXT.
Await the dataset agents, then quantify whether logged behaviour carries signal
that these 14 outputs cannot capture.

ENTRY 006  |  2026-08-10  |  The privacy section is not an appendix, it is a result
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
The paper needs a closing section on how an application would obtain the data
that L2, L3 and L4 require, with full user permission and without treating
people's health records carelessly. The obvious way to write that section is as
boilerplate: a paragraph about consent, a paragraph about encryption, a nod to
privacy legislation, and nothing measured.

That would be a waste, because the personalization ladder already answers the
question quantitatively.

THE INSIGHT.
Each rung of the ladder costs more personal data than the one below it.

    L0  nothing at all
    L1  age, sex, a self-reported activity level         (one onboarding form)
    L2  height, weight, waist, health conditions          (body measurements and
                                                           medical disclosure)
    L3  a daily behaviour log over weeks                  (continuous self-report
                                                           or a wearable)
    L4  within-day context, timing, location, mood        (passive, continuous,
                                                           the most intrusive)

So measuring the benefit at each rung is simultaneously measuring the privacy
cost of that benefit. The ladder is a privacy-utility curve. If most of the
achievable gain arrives at L3 and L4 adds little, then the honest engineering
recommendation is to stop at L3 and never collect the intrusive context data at
all. That is a stronger privacy statement than any policy paragraph, because it
is an argument from evidence that the data is not needed.

DECISION.
Write the final section as "What to collect, and what not to", built on the
measured curve, and structure it around data minimization as a testable
principle rather than a slogan. It will cover:

  1. The measured curve. Benefit per rung against what each rung requires a
     person to disclose. Name explicitly any feature that our experiments show
     does not earn its place, and recommend not collecting it.
  2. Consent that is specific and revocable. Purpose-bound per data category,
     not a single blanket agreement at signup, with the app degrading to a lower
     rung rather than breaking when a person declines or withdraws.
  3. Where the computation happens. Which parts of the ladder can be computed on
     the user's own device, so that raw logs never leave it, and which genuinely
     need a server.
  4. Storage and access. Row-level security so a row is readable only by the
     person it belongs to, encryption in transit and at rest, and separation of
     identifiers from health measurements.
  5. Retention and deletion, including what happens to a trained model when a
     person withdraws.
  6. The legal and ethical frame. The authors are in British Columbia, so PIPEDA
     and BC PIPA govern the application, and any study conducted on app users
     would fall under TCPS 2 and require research ethics board approval at
     Thompson Rivers University. GDPR matters if the app has users in Europe.
     State plainly that the current paper uses only public, de-identified,
     secondary data and required no ethics review, and that deploying the
     recommender to real users would.
  7. The limits of the recommender. It advises on behaviour; it does not
     diagnose. The book's referral triggers are the boundary, and the section
     will say where the app must stop and hand over to a clinician.

RATIONALE.
This is the section Dr. Anderson most needs, because he cannot ship the feature
without answering it, and it is the section a reviewer will look for hardest in
any health recommendation paper. Making it evidence-based rather than
aspirational is the difference between a paper that describes a system and a
paper that justifies one.

NEXT.
Nothing to do until the experiments produce the curve. Recorded now so the
experiment design deliberately measures benefit per rung in a way that supports
this section, rather than having to reconstruct it afterwards.

ENTRY 007  |  2026-08-10  |  Longitudinal data inventory, and a fabricated dataset
                             our own screen failed to catch
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
Twelve candidate longitudinal datasets were downloaded and measured. Six are
usable, six are not. The full report is at recon/datasets_longitudinal.md.

THE BACKBONE IS LIFESNAPS.
    71 people, median 88 days each, max 244, spanning 290 days
    behaviour, sleep, heart rate, HRV and SpO2
    SEMA momentary self-report on 2,290 person-days across 63 people
    validated psychometrics: PANAS, STAI, TTM, BREQ, Big Five
    an explicit adherence target: step_goal on 1,725 person-days, 59 people,
      hit rate 0.473, varying within 50 of the 71
It is the only dataset that is simultaneously multi-person, long, multi-modal,
and carries a real adherence signal. fitbit_mobius (33 people, 31 days, clean)
becomes the independent replication set.

A PRELIMINARY LADDER RESULT ALREADY APPEARS.
Forward-chaining prediction of next-day steps on LifeSnaps:
    L0, population mean            R2 = -0.019
    person's own mean              R2 =  0.262
    ridge on the person's history  R2 =  0.309
The population constant is worse than useless: a negative R2 means predicting
the population average beats nothing and loses to simply remembering what this
person usually does. On fitbit_mobius the person's own mean reaches R2 = 0.385.
This is the ladder's central claim appearing in the very first measurement, and
it needs to be re-derived carefully rather than accepted on one pass.

THE IMPORTANT METHODOLOGICAL FINDING.
whoop100k is fabricated. The evidence is overwhelming: exactly 100,000 rows
(286 users x ~350 days), every user starting 2023-01-01 with perfectly
contiguous calendars and zero absent days, sleep_performance constant at 100.0
for every row, workout_completed an exact function of activity_type, and nine
columns constant within every user.

But the decisive evidence is temporal, and this matters enormously to us:
    per-person lag-1 autocorrelation
      whoop100k resting HR   0.044      HRV   0.054
      LifeSnaps resting HR   0.850
      depresjon activity     0.896
A physiological state variable cannot be serially independent. Your resting
heart rate tomorrow is close to your resting heart rate today. In whoop100k it
is not, because each row was drawn independently. Out of sample, a person's own
history predicts their future strain at R2 = -0.003, versus +0.262 on LifeSnaps
and +0.385 on fitbit_mobius. There are no individuals in that file, only rows
wearing user IDs.

NOW THE UNCOMFORTABLE PART, AND IT IS A GENUINE CONTRIBUTION.
Our own fabrication screen, the one from the previous paper, did NOT catch this.
It returned REDUNDANT COLUMNS, a benign verdict. The screen tests whether a
target is an exact function of one other column, which is a cross-sectional
test. It has no concept of a person or of time, so a file can pass it while
containing no real individuals at all.

DECISION.
Extend the screen with a temporal integrity test and report it as a contribution
of this paper: for panel data, check whether per-person lag-1 autocorrelation of
physiological and behavioural state variables is distinguishable from zero, and
whether a person's own history predicts their future better than the population
mean does. Both are cheap, both are interpretable, and both would have caught
whoop100k immediately.

RATIONALE.
This is exactly the kind of finding that justifies the earlier work rather than
discarding it. The previous paper measured how often a cross-sectional screen
fires and found it about half precise. Here we find a fabricated dataset that
the same screen structurally cannot detect. That is an honest limitation of our
own prior method, discovered by us, and the fix is small and general.

TWO FURTHER DATA-INTEGRITY FINDINGS.
1. psykose is not an independent dataset. I verified this myself rather than
   taking the agent's word: 54 files are byte-identical between psykose and
   depresjon (168 distinct hashes in depresjon, 57 in psykose, 54 shared).
   Using both would silently double-count 54 subjects. Excluded.
2. The non-wear trap is real and differs by dataset, so it cannot be handled by
   one rule. In LifeSnaps 2,633 of 7,410 person-days (35.5%) have missing steps,
   and 2,256 of those report exactly 1440 sedentary minutes: non-wear surfaces
   as NaN, not zero, and only 86 days are literal zero-step days. In
   fitbit_mobius the opposite convention holds: 77 zero-step days, 72 of them
   with 1440 sedentary minutes, so there non-wear surfaces as a zero. Calories
   cannot be used to detect it because Fitbit emits basal metabolic rate on
   non-wear days (median 1,841 against 2,220 on worn days).
   Any analysis that treats these as real zeros will conclude that people are
   far more sedentary than they are. Filtering rule: drop person-days with
   sedentary_minutes == 1440 or missing steps, which retains 850 of 940 days in
   fitbit_mobius.

HOUSEKEEPING.
Deleted a 1.95 GB junk artifact (datasets/fitbit_mobius/_AGG_fitbit_mobius.csv)
left by the interrupted first pass: a blind concatenation of nineteen
incompatible schemas, fusing daily, hourly and minute-level columns into one
frame. Verified it was meaningless and unreferenced by any script before
removing. The datasets directory is now 2.0 GB.

NEXT.
Await the cross-sectional report, then fix the experiment design. The temporal
integrity test is now an experiment in its own right.

ENTRY 009  |  2026-08-10  |  E2 complete, after two failed detectors taught us
                             something worth reporting
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED, FIRST RUN.
The battery ran but produced two wrong answers, and both were instructive.

  1. D3 (temporal) returned lag-1 autocorrelation 1.0 for whoop100k, the
     fabricated dataset, when the truth is near zero. Cause: D3 took the maximum
     across candidate state variables, and weight_kg is stored as a constant
     repeated down every row of a person. A constant series has autocorrelation
     1.0, so a static attribute masked the dynamic ones entirely.
  2. D3 flagged fitbit_mobius, a REAL dataset, as fabricated. Cause: that file
     contains no physiological state variable at all, only behaviour. Day-to-day
     step counts are genuinely noisy in real people, so testing behaviour for
     persistence penalises honest data.
  3. D2 (signal) flagged LifeSnaps, also real, because it auto-selected a single
     target column that happened to be genuinely unpredictable.

DECISION AND FIX.
  D3 now applies only to physiological state variables, never behaviour, and
  requires at least ten distinct values within a person before a variable
  qualifies. If no qualifying variable exists the detector declares itself NOT
  APPLICABLE rather than flagging. This matters conceptually: a detector that
  cannot run must say so, not guess.
  D2 now tries up to eight plausible targets and keeps the best. The claim
  "nothing in this dataset predicts anything" is only credible if no candidate
  target works, not if one arbitrary column fails.

RESULT AFTER THE FIX.

  caught by D1 alone (published screen) 1
  caught by the full battery            5
  false alarms on real datasets         0 of 4

                          diet_rec_medical 0.5095 and fitness365 0.5272
    lag-1 autocorrelation lifesnaps 0.8618, against

                          95.37 percent of rows

INTERPRETATION.
Each detector catches a different dataset, and no single detector catches more
than three of five. That is the finding: fabrication is not one phenomenon, and a
single-question screen, including our own published one, will miss most of it.
The four detectors are cheap, require no labels, and produced no false alarms on
four real datasets.

A NOTE ON HONESTY.
D2 returns AUC 1.0 on whoop100k because that file contains a column exactly
determined by another. D2 only flags LOW signal, so it does not fire there; D3
catches it instead. I am recording this rather than tuning D2 to also flag
suspiciously high signal, because a high AUC is not by itself evidence of
fabrication, and a detector that fires on both extremes would be untestable.

person variance on LifeSnaps).

ENTRY 010  |  2026-08-10  |  E3 and E4: bodies are stable, behaviour is not
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

E3, NHANES 2013-2014, design-weighted with WTMEC2YR, jackknife CIs over the
15 strata x 2 PSU design. 6,113 adults, 5,587 with a measured waist. Median
weekly moderate-equivalent activity 150 minutes, with 33.1 percent reporting
none at all.

  Weighted R2                             BODY (WHtR)   BEHAVIOUR (weekly MVPA)
    L1   age + sex                            0.0791            0.0737
    L1+  + ethnicity + education + income     0.1047            0.0798
    L2   + BMI                                0.8947            0.0737
    L2+  + waist                                  -             0.0748
    L2++ + waist-to-height + blood pressure       -             0.0752

  Predicting who meets the activity guideline (cross-validated AUC)
    L1   age + sex                            0.6686
    L2   + BMI                                0.6686
    L2+  + waist                              0.6637
    L2++ + waist-to-height + BP               0.6657
    waist-to-height alone                     0.5868
    BMI alone                                 0.5366
    age alone                                 0.6497

THE RESULT.
Adding body measurements to age and sex improves prediction of the BODY enormously,
from 0.079 to 0.895, and improves prediction of BEHAVIOUR by nothing at all. The
MVPA R2 is 0.0737 with and without BMI, identical to four decimal places. The
adherence AUC is 0.6686 with and without BMI, also identical, and adding waist
makes it slightly WORSE (0.6637).

This is climbing from L1 to L2, the single most obvious improvement a developer
would make, and for behaviour it buys precisely nothing.

It also replicates the earlier project's NHANES 2021-2023 finding on a different
cycle collected years apart, with the same ordering: waist-to-height (0.5868)
beats BMI (0.5366) as a single predictor. Age alone (0.6497) beats both, which is
new and worth reporting.

A CIRCULARITY I CAUGHT AND FIXED.
The first run reported R2 = 0.9464 for predicting waist-to-height ratio from a
set containing waist. Waist-to-height IS waist divided by height, so that number
measures arithmetic, not explanation. Added a component guard that excludes any
predictor set containing a component of the target, and re-ran. Recording it
because it is exactly the sort of thing that survives into print if nobody
re-reads their own output.

E4, LifeSnaps, one-way random-effects ICC with a cluster bootstrap over people.

NON-WEAR FILTER FIRST. Of 7,410 person-days, 2,633 have a missing step count and
2,329 report exactly 1440 sedentary minutes, with 2,256 showing both. Only 86 are
literal zero-step days. Removing non-wear costs 2,706 rows (36.5 percent) and
leaves 4,704 days across all 71 people. Had these been treated as real zeros the
dataset would have appeared to contain a third more totally sedentary days than
it does.

  Intraclass correlation, the share of variance BETWEEN people
    minutesAsleep              0.202
    very_active_minutes        0.226
    moderately_active_minutes  0.232
    steps                      0.288
    sedentary_minutes          0.408
    calories                   0.571
    ---
    stress_score               0.265
    spo2                       0.324
    nightly_temperature        0.438
    rmssd                      0.708
    sleep_efficiency           0.785
    resting_hr                 0.866

    median ICC, behaviour   0.260
    median ICC, physiology  0.573

THE RESULT, AND IT IS THE SPINE OF THE PAPER.
Bodies are stable. Behaviour is not.

Resting heart rate is 87 percent a property of the person. Steps are 29 percent a
property of the person and 71 percent a property of the day. So a static profile,
no matter how much it knows, is mathematically capped at about a quarter of the
variance in what somebody actually does.

This explains E3 rather than merely accompanying it. Demographics and
anthropometrics predict the body well because the body is a stable person-level
trait. They predict behaviour badly because behaviour mostly is not a person-level
trait at all. The two experiments are the same finding seen from two directions,
one cross-sectional in a national survey of 6,113 adults, one longitudinal in 71
people wearing trackers.

AN HONEST NEGATIVE INSIDE E4.
My crude history features did NOT beat the person mean for behaviour in sample:
steps went 0.294 for the person mean, 0.310 adding day of week, but 0.226 for a
naive equal blend with yesterday and 0.246 for a trailing seven-day mean. I am
recording this as a limitation of the feature, not a finding about history,
because during acquisition a properly fitted ridge on a person's own history
reached 0.309 out of sample against 0.262 for the person mean. The lesson is that
history helps only when its weighting is learned rather than assumed, and E5 must
fit it rather than hardcode it.

For physiology the same crude lag-1 feature DOES help immediately: resting heart
rate goes from 0.866 to 0.948. Yesterday's resting heart rate is nearly all of
today's.

  This is the data-minimization argument in its strongest form: do not collect

AN IMPORTANT QUALIFICATION I MUST NOT LET THE PAPER OVERSTATE.
"Demographics do not help" is a statement about PREDICTING BEHAVIOUR. It is not a
statement that an app should stop collecting age and sex. Age determines the
heart-rate zones, the screening questions, and several contraindications in the
book's own safety rules. Sex and age drive the food-group targets. Those are
SAFETY and DOSING functions, and they are exactly where expert knowledge belongs.
The finding is narrower and sharper than "demographics are useless": demographics
tell you what is SAFE and APPROPRIATE for someone, and tell you almost nothing
about what they will actually DO. A recommender needs both, from different
sources. The paper must make this distinction explicitly or it will be read as
advocating that a health app discard the very variables its safety layer runs on.

ENTRY 012  |  2026-08-10  |  We were scooped on the taxonomy, and the paper has to say so
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
I claimed the personalization ladder as a contribution. I asked an agent to try
hard to disprove that, with explicit instructions to give an honest answer rather
than a reassuring one. It found prior work.

Abbasian and Jain, "Personal Care Utility", arXiv:2510.22602, posted 26 October
2025, contains a Table 1 captioned "Levels of personalization" defining a
six-rung ladder modelled on the SAE levels of driving automation. The
correspondence is close:

    theirs L0 generic broadcast          = our L0 population constant
    theirs L1 demographic (age/gender)   = our L1, effectively verbatim,
                                           including the exemplar variables
    theirs L2 behavioural tailoring      = our L3 own behaviour history
    theirs L3 + L4 situational and state = our L4 context
    theirs L5 co-regulated companionship = out of our scope
    (no counterpart)                     = our L2 anthropometrics

The idea also has older ancestors: the mass, targeted, tailored trichotomy from
health communication (Kreuter 1999; Hawkins 2008), a weak versus strong
personalization depth axis (Oinas-Kukkonen 2022), an ordinal weak / intermediate
/ strong-adaptive coding of 61 digital mental health systems (Matthews and
Rhodes-Maquaire 2025), and a static / dynamic / hybrid cut (Alslaity 2023).

DECISION.
Change the framing. We do not propose the ladder; we measure it. The paper will
state the prior work plainly in Related Work, including the arXiv preprint, and
reposition the contribution as the missing empirical ordering.

RATIONALE.
This is not a close call. A reviewer who finds that preprint and sees us claiming
the structure as new would be entitled to reject on that basis alone, and would
be right to. Finding it ourselves and citing it costs us a sentence of ambition
and buys the paper its credibility. It is also simply true, which settles it.

What survives is stronger than what we lose. Nobody has scored the marginal
predictive value of each personalization layer in one study, on one cohort, with
one metric. Three independent sources say so in their own words: ten Klooster
(2024) writes that effectiveness conclusions are impossible "because all the
forms are put together"; Matthews (2025) reports the trials reviewed "lacked a
non-personalised intervention, making it difficult to determine the added value";
and Abbasian and Jain disclaim their own table as "not prescriptive but
illustrative", with no data, model, or validation behind it.

A COUNTER-ARGUMENT WE MUST PRE-EMPT.
Hawkins (2008) explicitly rejects discrete levels in favour of continua. A
reviewer from health communication will raise this. Our answer is defensible and
must be written into the paper rather than left implicit: our rungs are cut by
the AVAILABILITY OF AN INPUT CLASS, which is a binary engineering fact about a
deployment, not by degree of audience segmentation, which is a continuum. Whether
a system holds the user's own logged history is a yes or no question.

TWO MORE CORRECTIONS FORCED BY THE SAME PASS.

1. Prior work on next-day activity prediction DOES exist. My earlier statement
   that it did not was wrong. Abdulghani et al. (Bioengineering 2026) predict
   next-day steps in 3-hour segments at MAE 981 and report no R2 at all. Fellger
   et al. (IEEE JTEHM 2020) reach NRMSE 9.11% and r = 0.89, but for active
   minutes, and pooled leave-one-out across 44 participants, which inflates the
   apparent fit with between-person variance: exactly the record-wise design
   Saeb (2017) warns against and exactly the error our grouped splits avoid.
   No peer-reviewed paper reports a within-person out-of-sample R2 for next-day
   step count. Our +0.23 therefore cannot be honestly benchmarked, and the paper
   will say that rather than implying it is a record.

2. Adams et al. (2017) found STATIC goals outperformed adaptive goals on steps
   (+2,630 versus +2,149 per day). This cuts against the spirit of our result and
   must be cited. Our claim is about PREDICTION, not efficacy: predicting
   behaviour better does not automatically produce better behaviour change. That
   distinction belongs in the limitations, stated by us rather than discovered by
   a reviewer.

ALSO NOTED.
There is no JITAI taxonomy ranking tailoring variables by depth. Nahum-Shani
supports only a baseline versus time-varying distinction, so we must not
overclaim it as a levels framework.

NEXT.
Rewrite the abstract, introduction, related work, and limitations. Then the two
review passes.

ENTRY 013  |  2026-08-10  |  The citation review, and the worst error in the paper
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

OBSERVED.
A reviewer agent checked every citation against the work it was attached to.
Bibliography metadata came back clean: all 84 DOIs resolve to the right work, no
wrong years, venues, volumes, pages or authors, no duplicates, no preprint
passed off as peer reviewed. The claims attached to them did not fare as well.
Ten citations were classified UNSUPPORTED. Verdict: do not submit.

THE WORST ONE, AND IT WAS IN OUR OWN HONESTY PARAGRAPH.
I wrote, in Limitations, that "in a twelve-month trial, adaptive goals derived
from a participant's own recent history were outperformed on daily steps by
static goals". Every part of that is wrong or misleading:
  - it is a FOUR-month trial, n = 96, not twelve months
  - the 2,630 versus 2,149 gap is NOT SIGNIFICANT (p = 0.095) and I did not say so
  - the trial's own stated conclusion is the OPPOSITE: "Adaptive goals
    outperformed static goals over a 4-month period", because the adaptive arm
    decayed at less than half the rate
  - the twelve-month trial I was half-remembering is a different paper by the
    same group, and it also favours adaptive goals

So the single concession I wrote against my own thesis, the paragraph designed to
show intellectual honesty, inverted its source. It is refuted by an open-access
abstract in under five minutes. That is worse than having no concession at all,
and it is the kind of error that destroys a reviewer's trust in everything else.

THE SECOND WORST.
I accused Fellger et al. of a record-wise evaluation design while arguing that
evaluation design matters. Their Methods say verbatim that they "excluded all
periods from training that were collected from the same participant", which is
subject-wise, the design Saeb recommends. My criticism of their POOLED METRICS is
correct and sufficient on its own; the design accusation was not. Being wrong
about another group's methods in a paper about methodological care is
self-refuting, and a reviewer could plausibly be one of their five co-authors.

THE THIRD.
Two of the three citations I used to justify forward chaining argue AGAINST it.
Bergmeir and Benitez recommend blocked cross-validation over reserving the end of
a series; Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo show k-fold performs favourably compared with
out-of-sample evaluation for autoregressive models. I had cited them as support
for the protocol they qualify.

A PATTERN WORTH RECORDING.
Five of the ten sit in Related Work. The reviewer's diagnosis is exact: that
section read as though citations were selected by topic and then attached to
whichever sentence needed a bracket, rather than read and matched to the claim.
A five-page vision paper was called a recent survey; one author group was
credited with another's taxonomy; a paper arguing for continua was called a
staged grading. Individually small, collectively fatal to the section's
authority.

AND TWO THINGS CITED NOWHERE.
Our own prior screen, which the whole of Section IV is built on, was referred to
four times and never cited. The book that Section III audits was never cited
either. Both are now in the bibliography and labelled as unpublished.

DECISION AND ACTION.
All ten rewritten. Two of the fixes made the paper better rather than merely
correct:
  - The Adams sentence is stronger accurate than it was wrong. Reporting the
    non-significance, the decay difference, and the authors' own conclusion is a
    more credible limitation than the false one I had.
  - The 2019 Canadian food guide could not certify the engine's age-and-sex
    serving table because that document ABOLISHED per-demographic serving counts.
    Cited correctly it becomes evidence FOR the paper's thesis: national dietary
    guidance abandoned demographic serving tables for the same reason we measure.
    I was throwing away an on-thesis point by citing it wrongly.

I also added regression guards to the verification script: the three corrected
sentences are now asserted, and the three wrong ones are banned by exact string,
so no later edit can silently reintroduce them. 91 of 91 checks pass.

WHAT I TAKE FROM THIS.
Every number in this paper was verified by machine and every one was right. The
errors were all in prose ABOUT sources, which no numeric check can catch. The
lesson is that a claim-to-source review is not optional polish; it is the only
thing that catches this class of error, and it caught a paper-destroying one.
